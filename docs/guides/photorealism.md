# ASTROWORLD: Taking a step further, photorealism

## Summary

The photorealism stage took the project a step further after Checkpoint 4, carrying out the Checkpoint 4 report's recommendations to make the real-time park look like a real theme park. Phong lighting was replaced with physically based Cook–Torrance shading, with every material described by how metallic and rough it is, and the scene gained real-time sun shadows through shadow mapping, high-dynamic-range lighting, ACES filmic tone mapping and bloom. The head was rebuilt as a single sculpted inflatable surface with a real mouth that visitors walk through, and the park grew into a walled site with physics-driven rides, such as a wave swinger, a pendulum pirate ship, teacups and bumper cars, along with trees, stalls, night lights and about 140 walking visitors. GPU instancing cut the drawing instructions per frame from about 2,750 to about 300, so it all still runs in real time, and the ray tracer was updated to match as a ground-truth reference.

## In detail

The photorealism stage takes the project a step further after Checkpoint 4. Its goal was to make the real-time 3D park look like a real ASTROWORLD theme park, and at the same time to carry out the recommendations from our own Checkpoint 4 report. That report admitted several weaknesses: the 3D head looked blobby and its mouth was only a glowing panel, the real-time version had no shadows, and it used a simplified lighting model. This stage fixes all of those, and it does so in real time on the graphics card, not in a slow offline render. It also makes the park much bigger and fills it with life. The giant gold inflatable head from the album cover becomes the park's entrance, and visitors walk in through its mouth.

The first big change is to how surfaces react to light. Up to this point we used the Phong lighting model, which is a convincing approximation but isn't based on physics. This stage replaces it with physically based rendering, using the Cook–Torrance model that modern games and films use. It treats every surface as millions of microscopic mirrors and works out three things. The distribution term says how many of those tiny mirrors point the right way to send light into your eye: on rough surfaces they are scattered, giving broad, soft highlights, and on smooth ones they are aligned, giving small, sharp highlights. The geometry term says how many of them are hidden in each other's shadows. The Fresnel term captures the way every surface becomes more mirror-like when seen at a grazing angle, which is why a distant road can look shiny. Each material is described by just two numbers: how metallic it is and how rough it is. The head, for example, is gold vinyl that is highly metallic and fairly smooth. It also reflects a blurred picture of its surroundings, so like the real inflatable in the album photo, its golden surface shows the blue sky and the plaza around it.

The second big change is real-time shadows, made with a technique called shadow mapping. Before each frame is drawn, the scene is drawn once more from the sun's point of view, recording only how far each point is from the sun. That produces a detailed depth image of 4096 by 4096 pixels. It uses a parallel, orthographic projection, because sunlight arrives in parallel rays. Then, as each pixel of the real image is drawn, the renderer asks whether anything stands between that point and the sun. If something closer to the sun was recorded there, the point is in shadow. Neighbouring samples are averaged so shadow edges are soft, and the comparison is nudged slightly to avoid "shadow acne", the speckled false shadows caused by a surface shadowing itself, a problem we had also solved in the ray tracer.

The third change is how light is handled across the whole image. Real light has an enormous range: at night a bulb is thousands of times brighter than a wall, and a normal image can't store that. So the scene is now lit in high dynamic range, in a buffer where brightness can go far beyond the normal maximum. All the lighting maths is done in linear light, as physics requires, and only converted to the screen's colour format at the very end. ACES filmic tone mapping then squeezes the huge range of brightness onto the screen the way camera film does, instead of clipping bright areas to flat white. Bloom picks out the brightest parts of the image, blurs them and adds them back, creating the glow around lights that you see through a real camera lens, so the lamps, ride bulbs and glowing mouth seem to shine at night. Exposure control and a subtle darkening at the edges of the frame complete the photographic look.

The head itself was completely rebuilt. Instead of separate rounded shapes stuck onto a face, it is now one continuous sculpted surface. The nose, the pouting upper lip, the closed eyelids with their creases, the cheeks and the brow are made by pushing the surface outward or inward in smooth bumps, so they flow into each other like inflated vinyl. Faint seams run down the sides and back, as on a real inflatable, and the jaw widens so the head stands solidly on the ground. Most importantly, the mouth is now a real opening: the arch is cut through the front and back of the head and joined by a glowing tunnel, so visitors can walk right through. The dreads are fat, inflated tubes that arch over the crown, curl at the tips and sway in the wind.

The park around the head grew into a believable, living place. The head now stands in a palisade wall as the entrance gate. In front of it is the album cover's plaza, with the rocket, the gold statue, the wrecked car, bunting and a balloon vendor, and a separate exit gate in the wall. Inside, alongside larger versions of the classic rides, a 30-metre Ferris wheel, the coaster, a 40-metre drop tower, the carousel and the big top, there are new rides, each moved by real physics or nested rotations. On the wave swinger, the chairs swing outward to exactly the angle where the chain's pull balances gravity against the spinning motion, following tan θ = ω²r/g; as the chairs swing out the radius grows, which changes the angle again, so the angle is found by repeating the calculation until it settles. The pirate ship swings as a true pendulum, with its timing set by T = 2π√(L/g), and its swing builds up and dies away over each ride. The teacups spin with three nested rotations, of the platform, the turntables and the cups themselves, and each bumper car follows its own looping Lissajous path, always facing the way it is moving.

Around the rides there are food stalls, about 85 trees, lawns, lamp posts along the paths and a meadow that stretches to the horizon. Around 140 visitors of different heights, clothing and skin tones walk a smooth looping route: in through the mouth, around the rides, out through the exit gate and back across the plaza. Their legs move according to the distance they have walked rather than the time, so their feet never slide on the ground. Visitors queue at the stalls and riders fill every ride. At night, lamp posts, ride lights and warm uplights at the foot of the head switch on with the day-night cycle, echoing the night version of the album artwork, and the renderer handles up to 16 nearby lights at once.

All of this had to stay fast enough to run in real time. With about 1,400 objects in every frame, the stage uses GPU instancing: all copies of the same shape are sent to the graphics card in a single instruction, together with a list of their positions. That cut the number of drawing instructions per frame from about 2,750 to about 300. On the laptop's integrated graphics, the park runs at roughly 28 frames per second by day and 22 at night. Measurements showed that the limit is now the graphics card's per-pixel work rather than the program itself, so a dedicated graphics card would run it much faster. The ray tracer from Checkpoint 4 was updated to use the same physically based materials, lights, sky and tone mapping, so it remains a ground-truth reference that the real-time image can be compared against.

The stage is honest about its limits too. The visitors and trees are still built from simple shapes, and because the head is sculpted by code rather than modelled by hand, its face is softer than the photographed inflatable. Those limits led directly to the next step, which brings in Blender.

In short, the photorealism stage turns the Checkpoint 4 report's list of what didn't work into things that do. The park now has physically based materials, real-time sun shadows, high-dynamic-range lighting with filmic tone mapping and bloom, and a single sculpted inflatable head with a mouth you can walk through. All of it sits in a much larger park full of physics-driven rides and visitors, and it still runs in real time.
