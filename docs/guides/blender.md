# ASTROWORLD: Taking a step further, Blender

## Summary

The Blender stage, the latest work, took the project a step further by keeping our C++ engine as the brain of the park and using Blender as the camera. An exporter samples our shared park description over 720 frames and writes every shape, every object's movement, tint and glow, and the lighting timeline, and Python scripts rebuild the scene in Blender and play the animation back frame by frame. Blender adds physically based materials, a physical sky with a real sunset, volumetric searchlights, motion blur, depth of field and a cinematic camera, and the head was sculpted by hand to match the real inflatable. The park also gained a closed-circuit roller coaster on a closed Catmull–Rom spline, with energy-based speeds and a lap of exactly 30 seconds, so the finished 30-second film loops seamlessly.

## In detail

The Blender stage is the latest work in ASTROWORLD, and it takes the project a step further on top of the photorealism stage. By then the park looked about as realistic as our own real-time renderer could manage on a laptop's graphics card. A few things still held it back: the face of the head, sculpted by code, was softer than the real inflatable, and real-time techniques can't fully match film-quality effects such as soft beams of light in haze, the blur of a camera lens or the streaking of fast motion. The idea of this stage was to keep our own C++ engine as the "brain" of the park while letting Blender, a professional 3D production tool, act as the "camera". Our code still decides every shape, the position of every object at every moment, the physics of every ride and the timeline of light from day to night. Blender only does the final, highest-quality rendering. Professional studios work the same way, using one tool to drive the animation and another to render it.

The work happens in three steps. First, a new program, the exporter, runs our existing park description, the same one the OpenGL program and the ray tracer use. It samples the park 24 times a second for 30 seconds, giving 720 frames. It writes every shape once, such as the head, a visitor's body or a gondola, and for every frame it records every object's position and orientation, its colour tint and how brightly it glows. It also writes the lighting for every frame, including where the sun is, the sky colours, the lamps and ride lights, the exposure and how far the dreads sway, along with the textures. Second, a Python script running inside Blender reads those files and rebuilds the scene. It creates each shape once and shares it between all of its copies, so all 140 visitors use one body shape, the same idea as the instancing in the previous stage. Third, a small script runs every time the frame changes. It looks up that frame in the exported data and moves, tints and lights every object to match, even blending between frames for smooth motion blur. This script is stored inside the Blender file itself, so anyone who opens the file in Blender and presses play sees the whole park come to life.

On top of that, Blender adds what it does best. The materials are physically based: gold vinyl with a glossy clear coat for the head, chrome, and textured paving. A physical sky simulates how sunlight scatters through the atmosphere, so the sky truly turns orange at sunset as our timeline lowers the sun, and at night it switches to our own purple, starry sky from the album artwork. The searchlights become spotlights inside cones of glowing haze, a volumetric effect that neither our real-time renderer nor our ray tracer could produce. Finally, a carefully designed camera flies through the park, with bloom around bright lights, motion blur like a real camera shutter, depth of field and filmic colour handling.

The one part of the park that didn't come from our code is the head. The head, its dreads and the tunnel through its mouth were sculpted by hand with Blender's sculpting tools and saved in the project as a separate file. The sculpture still had to follow our engine's rules: the mouth had to be big enough for the visitors' walking route to pass through, because that route is calculated by our C++ code. When the scene is built, the script swaps the sculpted pieces into the existing objects, so they keep their gold material, their glow and their animation, and the dreads get a gentle wind-sway so they move in the breeze. The result is a head far closer to the real inflatable from the album cover.

This stage also made a significant improvement to the park itself: a closed-circuit roller coaster. The old coaster was essentially the 2D side view from the early checkpoints lifted into 3D, so the train rose out of the ground and vanished at the far end. It is now a complete loop in one corner of the park. The train leaves the station, is pulled up a chain lift to 24 metres, plunges into the first drop and a climbing turn, rides a series of camelback hills, sweeps around a turn back towards the start, slows on a brake run and glides back into the station. The track is defined by 15 control points joined into a closed Catmull–Rom spline, the same kind of curve from Checkpoint 1, but closed so that the end flows smoothly into the start. The train moves slowly through the station and at a steady pace up the chain lift. After the top, its speed comes from conservation of energy, the height it has dropped minus a small loss to friction, so it is fastest at the bottom of the first drop, about 21.5 metres per second or 77 km/h, and slows on every hill. On the brake run its speed is gradually capped so it eases gently back into the station. One full lap takes exactly 30 seconds, the length of the animation, so the film loops seamlessly.

Making all of this work taught some practical lessons. Applying smoothing to each of the roughly 500 visitor body parts separately made every frame take about 150 seconds to render, while smoothing the shared shapes just once brought a full-quality frame down to about 16 to 34 seconds. Filling the entire world with haze turned the night scene completely black, whereas adding haze only where it was needed, in the searchlight beams and the mouth, gave the same effect cheaply. And because Blender ran on Windows while the project lived on Linux, reading data across the two systems was slow, so the data is read in one go.

The finished 30-second film, 720 frames at 24 frames per second, took about two and a half hours to render. It also showed that the approach works: change a ride in the C++ code, export again, and the film changes with it.

In short, the Blender stage ends the project's journey by connecting our own engine to a professional production renderer. Our C++ code remains the single source of truth for the park's shapes, motion, physics and lighting timeline, and Blender turns that into film-quality images, with realistic materials, a physical sunset, volumetric searchlights, a hand-sculpted walk-through head, motion blur and a cinematic camera. The rebuilt roller coaster runs on a closed, physics-timed circuit that loops perfectly with the film. It is a long way from the first checkpoint, where every pixel of a line was placed by hand.
